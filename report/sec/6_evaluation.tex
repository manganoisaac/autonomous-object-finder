\section{System Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

% Rubric: experimental evaluation, results and discussion (8)
\subsection{Protocol}
We evaluate the full system in two ways. \emph{(i) Student missions:} the three provided worlds \texttt{training\_start\_\{A,B,C\}} as supplied, with the supplied mission target (\texttt{soda\_can}, displayed at station S1). \emph{(ii) Monte Carlo benchmark:} 3 starts $\times$ 8 targets $\times$ 4 station layouts $=$ 96 missions per controller version. A layout is a cyclic re-assignment of the eight target images to the eight stations, applied by a supervisor before the robot moves, so each target is searched for at several stations and from every start. All runs use headless Webots R2025a on a CPU cluster. An independent referee supervisor, which never communicates with the controller, scores each run against the assignment criteria: success requires stopping within 0.20\,m of the correct observation position, with no contact other than the wheels on the floor, within 240\,s of simulation time. The referee also records completion time, final distance and the station the robot stopped at. The controller uses no ground truth.

\subsection{Results}
The final controller completes all three student missions (\cref{tab:missions}) and all 96 benchmark missions (\cref{tab:mc}) with no collision. The slowest mission takes 162\,s, leaving a 78\,s margin to the time limit; per start, the median and maximum times are 80/157\,s (A), 70/141\,s (B) and 73/162\,s (C). The student missions are slower than the benchmark median because S1 comes late in every start's tour (\cref{fig:route}). \Cref{fig:traj} checks that the robot executes what the planner intends: the logged trajectory stays within a median 2--3\,cm of the replayed A* route, and the largest deviation, 16\,cm, builds up on Start~C's 1.6\,m final straight, where heading drift is not corrected within a move.

\Cref{tab:mc} also shows how the system developed. Every version, including V0, \emph{found} the correct station in all 96 runs (median closest approach 0.036\,m). The differences come entirely from what happens after recognition. V1 fixed a stop bug that turned V0's 96 correct identifications into 0 successes (see below). Removing the step-back in V2 brought the median final distance from 0.110\,m to 0.038\,m, but the slow scan raised the worst case to 229\,s, only 11\,s inside the limit. V3 restored the time margin and kept V2's precision (\cref{fig:mc}).

\subsection{Failure cases}
\lead{Collisions after a correct identification (V0).}V0 identified the target correctly in every run but failed all 96: 92 by collision and 4 by timeout. After recognition it called a step-back routine that set both wheels to reverse and returned \emph{without} stopping them. Because \texttt{main()} then returned, the motors kept their last command, and the robot reversed across the arena until it hit an obstacle (median final distance 1.88\,m). Stopping the motors and idling the controller after the mission (V1) fixed every run.
\lead{Detector confusion.}The first detector, a ResNet18 classifier, confused \texttt{backpack} with \texttt{running\_shoe} in early integration tests. Re-scoring it on the captured frames shows a systematic bias rather than a single mistake. Its most frequent errors send dark posters to \emph{headphones} or \emph{running shoe} (camera$\to$headphones 79 times, headphones$\to$shoe 49, backpack$\to$shoe 40, over 1{,}024 frames), even at square poses (\cref{fig:qual}a). This motivated the encoder study and the view-matched augmentation of \cref{sec:perception}.
\lead{Near-timeout runs.}V2's slow scan pushed 8 of 96 missions beyond 200\,s. No run failed, but the margin was too small for a live demonstration, which led to the faster scan in V3.
\lead{Stuck-detector false positives.}The initial stuck detector classified the robot as STUCK if the cumulative displacement over the last 10 steps was less than 70\% of the expected displacement. 
However, this approach generated 10 false positives during the Start A/B/C normal missions. 
Analysis of debug logs showed that immediately after turning to a heading of $\pm\pi$, even during normal operation, the step displacement alternated between approximately 2\,mm and 4\,mm, and the cumulative criterion misinterpreted this temporary fluctuation as a stop. 
Nevertheless, during normal operation, such slow steps occurred at most four times in a row.
Accordingly, the decision rule was redesigned to check whether any single step among the most recent 8 steps exceeded 3.0\,mm (Rule A in \cref{sec:safety}), rather than using the cumulative criterion.
After the modification, the number of false-positive STUCK events during normal missions decreased to 0, and STUCK detection was maintained for both head-on and 45$^\circ$ approaches during fault-injection tests with the proximity sensor input disabled.

\subsection{Ablations}
\Cref{tab:ablation} isolates three design choices on the same 96 missions. \emph{Single frame:} the 4-frame scan is replaced by a single frame at the observation yaw. \emph{Perturbed start:} the supervisor shifts the start pose by up to $\pm5$\,cm in $x$ and $y$ and $\pm15^\circ$ in heading (uniform, fixed seed per run). \emph{Safety off:} the same perturbed missions with proximity monitoring and stuck detection disabled.

\lead{Scan.}A single frame is as accurate as the 4-frame scan on all 96 missions and saves 3.5\,s per mission (paired median). The planner delivers the robot almost square to each station, within the single-frame tolerance of \cref{sec:perception}. We keep the scan because it costs little and covers larger arrival errors, where single-frame accuracy drops (\cref{fig:multiview}).
\lead{Start perturbation.}Shifting the start pose changes the mission time by a paired median of $-0.4$\,s (maximum $+1.0$\,s). The first $90^\circ$ turn snaps the heading back to the grid, and GPS cells absorb the position offset.
\lead{Safety off.}With proximity monitoring and stuck detection disabled, all 96 perturbed missions are identical to the safety-on runs, to 0.01\,s of simulation time. The event logs confirm this directly: across 192 nominal missions over all eight layouts, the controller logged 0 proximity events and 0 stuck detections (\cref{tab:obstacle}). The planned paths alone kept every nominal run collision-free. The safety layers are therefore headroom for disturbances outside this benchmark (unmapped obstacles, wheel slip, a mis-set start). \Cref{sec:safety} evaluates them directly with fault injection.

\subsection{Unmapped obstacle and safety events}
\label{sec:obstacle}
To test the safety layers where they matter, the referee places a $6{\times}6{\times}8$\,cm box that is \emph{not} in the occupancy grid on a cell of the robot's first planned route, before the robot moves. It replays the controller's planner, draws the cell with the run's seed, and uses the same box in the ON and OFF runs. Safety events are counted from the controller's own log lines. We also re-ran the nominal benchmark over all eight layouts, so every target appears at every station.

\lead{Nominal, all layouts.} All 192 missions succeed (median 73\,s, worst 162\,s), with no safety event. With deterministic physics, mission time depends only on the start and the goal station, so layouts 4--7 reproduce the time distribution of layouts 0--3 exactly; vision never changes the path.

\lead{Safety off.} The planner drives straight into the box: every run collides, after a median of 9.7\,s.

\lead{Safety on.} The proximity layer stops the robot in front of the box in every run, and 54 of 90 completed missions (60\%) still succeed. The logs show how. After each FRONT event the robot reverses and replans, but the static map yields the same route, so it stops again. This repeats until the 100-retry limit (median 100 events per run), after which the robot abandons that station and continues the tour. The two remaining failure modes match the limitations in \cref{sec:discussion}. In 18 runs the robot, having given up, classifies the view from wherever it stands; because the closed-set decision always returns a label and the 0.20 threshold is not applied in the loop, it accepts a wrong station. The other 18 runs, all from Start~A, end in contact after about 200 repeated reverse-and-replan cycles. Stuck detection never fired: every event was caught by the proximity layer first.
